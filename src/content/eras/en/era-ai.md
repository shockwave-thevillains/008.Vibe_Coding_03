The idea of thinking machines is much older than the computer, but artificial intelligence (AI) as a research field was born alongside electronic computing. This chapter records its milestones without guessing at the future.

## The first questions

In 1950, **Alan Turing** published "Computing Machinery and Intelligence", which opens with the question "Can machines think?". Rather than define "thinking", he proposed a question-and-answer game now known as the Turing test. In the summer of 1956, John McCarthy and colleagues held a research workshop at Dartmouth College under the name *artificial intelligence*, an event often treated as the birth of the field.

## Booms and busts

The following decades swung between optimism and disappointment. Programs built on hand-written rules succeeded at narrow tasks but struggled with the messiness of the real world, and funding dried up in periods often called "AI winters". In 1997, IBM's chess computer **Deep Blue** beat world champion Garry Kasparov, a landmark for computational search in a game with clear rules.

## Learning from data

From the early 2010s, **artificial neural networks** trained on very large amounts of data and computing power made leaps in recognising images, speech and language. In 2017 the paper "Attention Is All You Need" introduced the **Transformer** architecture, which underpins many large language models. On 30 November 2022, ChatGPT was released and quickly brought **generative AI**, systems that produce text, images or code, to wide public attention.

In 2024, the Nobel Prize in Physics went to John Hopfield and Geoffrey Hinton for foundational discoveries enabling machine learning with artificial neural networks. The Nobel Prize in Chemistry went to David Baker, and to Demis Hassabis and John Jumper for protein structure prediction with AlphaFold.

## Rules and responsibility

As AI use spreads, governments have begun writing policy. Indonesia published its **National Strategy for Artificial Intelligence 2020–2045** in 2020. The **EU AI Act** entered into force on 1 August 2024, taking a risk-based approach. Questions of reliability, copyright, privacy and effects on work remain under active debate among researchers, policymakers and the public.
