From around the middle of the 18th century in Britain, the way people made things changed dramatically. The muscle power of people and animals, along with water and wind, began to give way to coal-driven machines. The change spread to continental Europe, North America and eventually the whole world.

## Steam and factories

Steam engines had pumped water out of mines since the early 18th century, but they burned fuel wastefully. In 1769, **James Watt** patented a separate condenser that made them far more efficient. Engines like this went on to drive textile mills, grinding mills and workshops. People moved from villages to industrial towns, often into harsh working conditions, children included.

## Rails and wires

In 1830 the **Liverpool and Manchester Railway** opened as the first intercity line to rely entirely on steam locomotives and run passengers to a timetable. A few decades later, telegraph cables crossed continents and ocean floors, and news could travel between continents in hours.

## Java in the industrial economy

Dutch industrialisation was partly paid for by Java. Under the **Cultivation System** (1830–1870), farmers were forced to grow coffee, sugar cane, indigo and other export crops; the profits flowed into the Dutch treasury while many farmers suffered. On 10 August 1867, the first railway in the Dutch East Indies opened between Semarang and Tanggung.

When **Krakatau** erupted in August 1883, news of the disaster was telegraphed from Batavia to Singapore and on to the rest of the world. Newspapers in London and San Francisco carried the story within a day: the world was connected in ways that would have been unimaginable a century earlier.
