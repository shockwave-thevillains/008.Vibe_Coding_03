"Classical antiquity" began as a European label for the Greek and Roman world. Here it is used more broadly, for roughly 800 BCE to 476 CE, when several great civilisations across Eurasia produced ideas that still carry weight today.

## Ideas that lasted

In Greece, small independent city-states became the birthplace of philosophy, history-writing and drama. In 508 BCE the reforms of Cleisthenes in Athens created what came to be called *demokratia*: free male citizens decided the city's affairs in an assembly. Women, enslaved people and foreigners were excluded.

In South and East Asia, the teachings of the Buddha and of Confucius spread. After the bloody Kalinga war, the Mauryan emperor **Ashoka** had edicts on *dhamma*, virtue and tolerance carved on pillars across his realm.

## Great empires

In 221 BCE, **Qin Shi Huang** unified China and standardised its script, coinage and units of measure. The Han dynasty that followed opened the overland trade routes later known as the Silk Road. In the west, Rome grew from a republic into an empire that ruled the entire Mediterranean coast. In 105 CE, Cai Lun, an official at the Han court, reported a papermaking method to the emperor; archaeology shows paper was made earlier, but his report marks the technique becoming standard.

## The archipelago enters the written record

On the banks of the Mahakam River in East Kalimantan, seven **yupa** were found: stone posts inscribed in Sanskrit using the Pallava script. They record the gifts of a king named Mulavarman and, based on the style of the letters, date to the second half of the 4th century CE. They are the oldest known inscriptions in Indonesia and early evidence of cultural and trade links with India.

By convention, the era ends in 476 CE, when the Western Roman Empire fell. The Eastern Roman Empire carried on for almost another thousand years.
