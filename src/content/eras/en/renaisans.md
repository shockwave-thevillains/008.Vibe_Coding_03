The Renaissance, or "rebirth", first described a revival of interest in ancient Greek and Roman works in the cities of Italy during the 14th and 15th centuries. From art and literature, that spirit spread to the way people questioned nature.

## The printing press

Around 1455, Johannes Gutenberg in Mainz printed a Bible using movable metal type. Within a few decades, presses had spread to hundreds of European towns. Books, maps and ideas could now be copied quickly and cheaply, including ideas that challenged old authorities.

## From observation to laws of nature

In 1543, **Nicolaus Copernicus** published a model that placed the Sun at the centre of the planetary system. In 1610, **Galileo Galilei** turned a telescope to the sky and reported mountains on the Moon and four moons circling Jupiter. The high point came in 1687, when **Isaac Newton** published the *Principia*: laws of motion and universal gravitation that explained a falling apple and a planet's orbit with the same mathematics. Historians call this chain of changes the **Scientific Revolution**.

## Spices and knowledge from Maluku

Nutmeg, cloves and mace from the Maluku Islands were among the most valuable goods in the world. In 1511 the Portuguese seized Malacca, and in 1602 the Dutch founded the **VOC**, a trading company that went on to control the spice trade, often by brutal means.

The period also produced knowledge about the archipelago. Georg Eberhard Rumphius, a naturalist living on Ambon, compiled the *Herbarium Amboinense*, describing around 2,000 plants with their local names, and finished it around 1690. It was only published in 1741–1750, long after his death, and became a reference for Linnaeus.
