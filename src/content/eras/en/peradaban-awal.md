From around the end of the fourth millennium BCE, some farming societies grew into large cities with rulers, armies, temples and merchants. The more complex a society became, the more it needed to keep track of things: who stored the grain, who owed what, who had to work.

## Writing as bookkeeping

In **Mesopotamia**, the city of Uruk produced thousands of marked clay tablets from about 3350–3000 BCE. Most are administrative records: quantities of goods, workers' rations, livestock. From these picture-like signs grew **cuneiform**, a script used for more than 3,000 years to write many different languages.

In **Egypt**, bone and ivory labels from tomb U-j at Abydos, around 3200 BCE, are among the oldest hieroglyphs. Writing also developed in the Indus Valley, whose script is still undeciphered, and in China, where inscriptions on Shang dynasty oracle bones from around 1250 BCE are the earliest form of Chinese script.

## States, monuments and law

The Great Pyramid of Giza, built for the pharaoh Khufu in the middle of the third millennium BCE, shows how far a state could organise labour. Around 1754 BCE, King Hammurabi of Babylon had 282 laws carved on a basalt stele. Laws set up in public were a powerful idea: rules that could be read, not just remembered.

## Letters for everyone

By around 1050 BCE, Phoenicians on the Levantine coast were using an alphabet of 22 consonant letters. With far fewer signs than cuneiform or hieroglyphs, it travelled along Mediterranean trade routes and became the ancestor of the Greek and Latin alphabets, among many others.
